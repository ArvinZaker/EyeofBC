Understanding access

Eye Health Equity

Access to eye exams, glasses, treatment and vision rehabilitation varies by geography, income and coverage across British Columbia and Canada.

The numbers

1.2 million

Canadians living with vision loss or blindness in 2019, 3.2% of the population [1]

8 million+

Canadians living with age-related macular degeneration, cataract, diabetic retinopathy or glaucoma [1]

13.8 million

projected for those four diseases by 2050, up from 8 million in 2019 [1]

$32.9 billion

estimated cost of vision loss in Canada in 2019 ([1] reports $15.6 billion in financial cost and $17.4 billion in lost wellbeing); $4.5 billion of it in British Columbia [1]

$56 billion

projected annual cost by 2050 [1]

Up to 40%

of people with definite glaucoma were unaware of their condition (Canadian Health Measures Survey, ages 40 to 79) [2]

25%

of Canadian youth have myopia, three times the rate in 1990 [3]

1 in 3

children and adolescents worldwide have myopia [3]

Why gaps exist

“Rural, remote, and isolated communities, Indigenous Peoples, newcomers, and low-income Canadians face disproportionate barriers, including provider shortages in some areas, travel distances, and affordability challenges.” [4]

Rural and remote

Barriers: Limited local professionals, travel distances, weather, appointment delays, repeat follow-up visits and limited access to low-vision rehabilitation.

What could help: Mobile eye care, teleophthalmology, visiting specialist clinics and referral pathways.

Indigenous peoples

Barriers: Geographic isolation, provider shortages, cost, transport and systemic discrimination; communities are diverse.

What could help: Indigenous-led initiatives, culturally safe care, local and visiting services, relationships and Indigenous involvement in design.

Newcomers and refugees

Barriers: Language barriers, an unfamiliar health system, cost, transport and coverage uncertainty.

What could help: Some eligible refugees and claimants may receive vision benefits through the Interim Federal Health Program (IFHP), where eligibility, co-payments, covered services and participating providers must be confirmed.

Low-income and housing insecurity

Barriers: Public insurance covers medically necessary physician services, while routine exams, glasses and contact lenses vary, alongside medication storage, appointment follow-up, disability access and lack of benefit information.

What could help: Shelter outreach, local optometrist partnerships, low-cost or donated eyewear, clear benefit information and care navigation.

Living with vision loss

Vision rehabilitation, assistive technology, orientation and mobility training and accessible materials help people with low vision stay independent. Workplace accommodations and community support connect people to local services; see Find Eye Care for CNIB and Vision Loss Rehabilitation Canada.
